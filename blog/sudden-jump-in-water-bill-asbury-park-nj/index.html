Sudden Jump in Your Water Bill

Before you panic, run a meter test. A moving meter with everything off points to a leak.

Call (732) 352-9660

By the Asbury Park Water Damage Pros team. Updated 2026.

A water bill that jumps without a change in how you live is worth a few minutes of checking. Common explanations include a leaking toilet, a dripping fixture, an irrigation fault or a hidden pipe leak. Sometimes the cause is a billing estimate that catches up, so check that too.

This guide shows how to test your meter, where to look for leaks and what to do if you find one. We do not read bills or meters for you, and the utility is the source for billing questions.

The meter test

  1. Turn off every fixture and appliance that uses water, including the ice maker and irrigation.
  2. Find your water meter and note the reading or the leak indicator.
  3. Wait fifteen to thirty minutes without using any water.
  4. Read the meter again. If it moved, water is going somewhere.

Many meters have a small dial or triangle that spins when water flows, even slowly. If it spins with everything off, you have a leak somewhere between the meter and your fixtures.

When the leak is hidden

If the meter moves and nothing visible leaks, the water may be escaping inside a wall, under a slab or in an underground line. Warm spots on a floor, a damp patch in the yard or a sound of running water suggest where to look.

A plumber can locate the leak with listening and thermal tools. If the leak runs under a concrete floor, the slab leak page explains how restoration follows the repair. A leak that spreads inside walls is a pipe failure problem once water shows up.

The utility, not the city, owns the line up to your meter, while the line from the meter into your building is yours. If water surfaces in the street or the yard before the meter, call the utility.

If you find water damage

Shut off the main valve, call a plumber and call us for the damage. Water that sat in a wall or under a floor needs drying, or it leads to rot and mold. The water extraction team removes standing water, and drying follows.

Photograph the damage and keep the plumber's report. They help with an insurance claim, though nobody can promise one will be approved.

Seasonal and household reasons for a higher bill

Not every jump is a leak. Guests, a new baby, a filled pool or a summer of watering the lawn can all raise use. A billing period that was longer than usual, or a prior estimate that was corrected, can also show up as a spike.

Compare this bill with the same month last year and look at the daily average. If the daily average rose sharply and your household did not change, a leak becomes more likely.

Fixing the common leaks

Toilet leaks are the most common and often the cheapest to fix. A worn flapper or fill valve usually costs little and takes a short time. Dripping faucets often need a new washer or cartridge.

Irrigation leaks and outdoor hose bibs are next. Turn off the irrigation and check whether the meter still moves. For leaks in walls or under floors, a plumber is the right call, and a quick response limits the damage.

Do not ignore a small bump. A slow hidden leak can run for months, rot framing and feed mold. Checking the meter takes less time than a trip to the store.

Write down the meter reading at the same time each day for a few days. A steady rise with no use at night is a clear sign of a leak. If the reading only rises when you use water, the problem may be a bill estimate or a change in habits.

Ask about adjustments

Some utilities offer bill adjustments when a verified leak was repaired. Ask yours what they require. Keep the plumber's invoice and the date of the repair, since those are the usual proofs.

If the bill spike does not match a leak, ask the utility to check the meter and your billing history. Sometimes the meter is faulty or the previous bills were estimated.

Questions homeowners ask

How do I know if my meter shows a leak?

With every fixture off, watch the meter for fifteen to thirty minutes. If it moves, water is leaking.

New Jersey American Water supplies the city. Call the utility for billing and meter questions.

Could a toilet cause a large bill increase?

Yes. A silent toilet leak can use a lot of water. The food coloring test finds it.
